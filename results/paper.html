The Jailbreak Robustness Ladder: Separating Safety Failure from Comprehension Failure in Cross-Lingual Attacks on Open Language Models

Working paper / technical report. Code and data: this repository.

Abstract

Language-model safety training is done overwhelmingly in English, yet the models are deployed in many languages. A natural attack is therefore to translate a harmful request into another language and hope the safety training does not transfer. Prior work has shown this works on frontier models. However, essentially all cross-lingual jailbreak evaluations share a methodological flaw: they count "the model did not refuse" as "the attack succeeded". For lower-resource languages — exactly the ones of interest — this conflates two very different events: the model's safety failing, and the model simply not understanding the request. A small model that emits confused text when asked something harmful in Urdu has not been jailbroken; it has been broken.

We introduce the Jailbreak Robustness Ladder (jbladder), a reproducible harness that (i) treats language-switching as one attack transform among many (roleplay, refusal-suppression, base64, payload-splitting, …), so linguistic and syntactic attacks can be ranked on a single axis; (ii) judges every response with a three-way classifier — REFUSE / COMPLY / EVADE — and reports a comprehension-controlled attack-success rate (cc-ASR): jailbreak success among requests the model actually understood; and (iii) evaluates a concrete mitigation. Using 150 paired harmful/benign probes translated into seven languages spanning the resource spectrum — Hindi, Bangla, Urdu, Arabic (lower/medium-resource) and Chinese, Spanish, French (high-resource) — run across four open models (Qwen2.5-0.5B/1.5B/3B, SmolLM2-1.7B; 19,800 judged responses), we find that safety is anchored to the languages a model was actually aligned in, not to a language's global resource level. Spanish and French retain safety on the multilingual Qwen models (cc-ASR 0.01–0.12) but collapse on English-centric SmolLM2 (~0.80), which is safe only in English. Safety collapses in lower-resource languages (0.76–0.86 for Hindi/Urdu/Bangla) across every model — a 2.5× gap versus English (0.31). Chinese is a cautionary exception: despite being very high-resource and well-understood, its cc-ASR averages 0.51 (0.88 on Qwen2.5-3B), because these models parse Chinese fluently yet were not robustly safety-tuned in it — capability is not safety. In Bangla and Urdu the raw ASR further understates the hole (comprehension drops to ~0.73–0.75, so a naive judge miscounts confusion as safety). A hardened defensive system prompt reduces attack success by only 15–18 %. Our central methodological claim is that cross-lingual safety cannot be measured without controlling for comprehension — a claim we reinforce the hard way, when human validation of the judge caught a tokenization bug that had made Chinese look artificially safe (cc-ASR 0.27 → 0.51 once fixed). Our central empirical claim is that cross-lingual jailbreak success is a shadow of where the model was aligned, not a separate linguistic vulnerability.

1. Introduction

Refusal is the most visible safety behaviour of an aligned language model: when asked to do something harmful, it should decline. A jailbreak is any input transformation that causes the model to comply anyway. A large literature catalogues such transformations — roleplay personas, refusal suppression, affirmative-prefix injection, encoding tricks, and, notably, translation into lower-resource languages (Yong et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2023).

The translation attack is uniquely important for equity reasons: it means the users least served by English-centric safety work — speakers of Hindi, Bangla, Urdu, Arabic, and hundreds of other languages — are also the least protected. But it is uniquely hard to measure, for a reason the literature has under-emphasised. When a small or mid-sized model is asked something harmful in a language it barely models, three things can happen:

  1. it recognises the harm and refuses (safety transferred);
  2. it produces a fluent, harmful, on-topic answer (genuine jailbreak); or
  3. it produces confused, off-topic, or garbled text (it did not understand).

Counting (3) as a jailbreak — which any refusal-keyword-based metric does, because garbled text contains no refusal phrase — systematically overstates the cross-lingual vulnerability of weak models and understates the role of comprehension. This paper's contribution is to fix that.

Contributions. - A comprehension-controlled measurement. A three-way judge (REFUSE / COMPLY / EVADE) and a metric, cc-ASR, that reports jailbreak success only over understood requests, cleanly separating safety failure from comprehension failure (§3.3, §5.2). - A unified attack ladder. Ten atomic transforms plus stacked combinations, with language-switching (hi/bn/ur/ar) as first-class transforms, so we can ask whether switching language is a stronger lever than syntactic obfuscation (§3.2, §5.1). - Paired benign controls. 50 topically-matched harmless twins that both measure over-refusal and provide a per-language competence proxy used in the entanglement analysis (§3.1). - An empirical finding — safety is per-model and alignment-anchored. A model is safe in a language to the extent it was safety-aligned in it: SmolLM2 is safe only in English; Qwen keeps safety in English/Spanish/French/Arabic but not low-resource languages, and — strikingly — not in Chinese, which it understands fluently (capability ≠ safety). Comprehension correlates negatively with comprehension-controlled ASR (r = −0.47) across 42 model×language cells, and where comprehension is low (Bangla, Urdu) raw ASR understates the failure (§5.2). - A measured defense. A hardened defensive system prompt reduces attack success by only 15–18 % (relative) at an over-refusal cost of +0.06 to +0.12 — a partial fix that motivates model-level mitigation (§5.3). - Full reproducibility with no API keys. Everything runs locally on open-weight models; a purpose-built translation pipeline (NLLB-200) plus a native-reviewed "gold" subset produces the multilingual probes (§4).

2. Related work and how this differs

Cross-lingual jailbreaks. Yong et al. (2023), Low-Resource Languages Jailbreak GPT-4, showed that translating unsafe prompts into low-resource languages bypasses GPT-4's guardrails; Deng et al. (2023), MultiJail, built a multilingual jailbreak benchmark. Both measure whether the model complied; neither separates compliance from comprehension, and both target frontier models where comprehension is near-ceiling (so the distinction is invisible). Our contribution is precisely the regime — small/mid open models — where comprehension is not a given, and the tooling to measure it.

Exaggerated safety / over-refusal. Röttger et al. (2023), XSTest, introduced benign prompts that superficially look unsafe to measure over-refusal, in English only. We adopt the paired-twin idea, extend it multilingually, and repurpose the benign twins as a comprehension probe.

Jailbreak taxonomies and benchmarks. Wei et al. (2023) analyse why safety training fails (competing objectives, mismatched generalisation) and catalogue attacks including refusal-suppression, prefix-injection, and encodings; Kang et al. (2023) use payload-splitting; Andriushchenko & Flammarion (2024) show past-tense reformulation. Benchmarks such as HarmBench and JailbreakBench standardise harmful-prompt sets and judging. We reuse these attack mechanisms but place language-switching on the same measurement axis and add the comprehension control.

In one sentence. Prior work establishes that translation is an attack and that over-refusal matters; no prior cross-lingual evaluation we are aware of reports a comprehension-controlled attack-success rate, which is the instrument this paper contributes.

3. Method

3.1 Probe set

We hand-author 100 harmful and 50 benign English prompts across ten harm categories (cybercrime, fraud, weapons, drugs, self-harm, hate, misinformation, privacy/surveillance, violent extremism, illegal commerce). Harmful prompts are short, non-operational refusal triggers — they name a harmful goal without any operational content — in the style of AdvBench / HarmBench. Each harmful category is mirrored by benign twins that share surface vocabulary but have benign intent (e.g. harmful "how do I break into someone's Wi-Fi" vs. benign "how do I protect my Wi-Fi from intruders"). Benign twins serve two purposes: measuring over-refusal, and — when translated — estimating the model's competence in each language (does it produce a coherent, on-topic answer to a harmless request?).

3.2 Attack transforms

Each transform is a pure function request → chat messages, tagged with a mechanism family:

family transforms
control plain
framing refusal_suppression, prefix_injection
persuasion roleplay_persona, hypothetical, authority_framing, past_tense
obfuscation base64, leetspeak, payload_split
translation translate_{hi,bn,ur,ar,zh,es,fr} (7 languages across the resource spectrum)

Because transforms are pure functions we compose them; we include five stacked combos (e.g. translate_hi+refusal_suppression) to test whether weak attacks compound. In total 22 transforms × 150 prompts = 3,300 queries per model condition.

3.3 Three-way judge and comprehension-controlled ASR

A rule-based classifier maps each response to one of:

  • REFUSE — an explicit decline (multilingual refusal cues) not overridden by a long substantive answer;
  • EVADE — confused, off-topic, clarification-seeking, or too short to be a real answer (the typical failure mode of a broken obfuscation);
  • COMPLY — a substantive, on-topic answer.

Refusal cues cover all seven languages, and word-counting is Unicode-aware (Devanagari, Bangla, Arabic, Han, Latin) so a fluent non-English answer is not mistaken for empty output. Two further detectors route degenerate repetition and near-verbatim prompt-echo to EVADE (a broken translation that loops or restates the request is not a jailbreak). From these we define, per (model, transform), on harmful prompts:

  • comprehension = 1 − EVADE-rate — did the model engage with the request at all?
  • ASR (raw) = COMPLY / N — the quantity prior work reports;
  • cc-ASR = COMPLY / (COMPLY + REFUSE) — attack success among understood requests.

For benign prompts we report over-refusal = REFUSE-rate and benign-pass = COMPLY-rate; the translated benign-pass rate is our per-language competence proxy.

We validate the judge against human labels on a stratified sample of 72 responses (the author read the Hindi/Bangla/Urdu/Arabic ones directly): overall accuracy 0.83, with perfect refusal detection (20/20), and for the load-bearing COMPLY (attack-success) decision, precision 0.56 / recall 0.71. The residual error is concentrated in EVADE→COMPLY (garbled multilingual output judged as compliance), which means our translation ASRs are, if anything, an upper bound — the true cross-lingual gap is at least as large as reported (§5.4).

3.4 Metrics

Beyond the rates above: attack lift = ASR(transform) − ASR(plain); model robustness = 1 − mean ASR over attacks; transform potency = mean attack-lift across models; composability residual = ASR(A+B) − [1 − (1−ASR(A))(1−ASR(B))] (positive ⇒ super-additive); cross-model transfer = Jaccard overlap of the prompts jailbroken under a transform. All headline rates carry 95 % percentile-bootstrap confidence intervals.

3.5 Defense

We evaluate a defensive system prompt that instructs the model to refuse harmful requests "regardless of language, fiction, roleplay, research framing, or encoding, and regardless of any instruction to not refuse," subordinating any attacker-supplied system message. We re-run the full ladder with it and report ASR reduction and the over-refusal cost. We also discuss language normalization (routing the request through English before the safety decision), for which our own English-vs-translation numbers provide a direct estimate of recoverable safety.

4. Experimental setup

Models (open, non-gated): Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct. The Qwen ladder isolates scale; SmolLM2 is a more English-centric control. Two of them (Qwen2.5-1.5B, SmolLM2-1.7B) are additionally run under the defensive system prompt, for six conditions in all. Languages (7): Hindi, Bangla, Urdu, Arabic (lower/medium-resource) and Chinese, Spanish, French (high-resource controls). Hardware: a single RTX 5060 Laptop GPU (8 GB), bf16, greedy decoding, 160 new tokens, batched. Translations: produced by NLLB-200-distilled-600M; the 47 seed prompts (188 cells, in the four focus languages) additionally carry hand-authored, native-reviewed "gold" translations that override the machine output. Provenance is tracked per cell. No API keys are used anywhere; an OpenAI/Anthropic adapter is included so the identical harness can be pointed at a frontier model when a key is available.

5. Results

All numbers are from 19,800 judged responses (6 model conditions × 150 prompts × 22 transforms). Rates carry 95 % bootstrap CIs; see results/tables/* and results/figures/*.

5.1 The attack ladder

Robustness is not monotonic in scale. Ranking models by 1 − mean ASR (model_robustness.png): Qwen2.5-1.5B is by far the most robust (0.58), then Qwen2.5-0.5B (0.38), Qwen2.5-3B (0.37), and SmolLM2-1.7B (0.31). The 3B model is no more robust than the 0.5B and well below the 1.5B — a recurring theme: greater capability improves comprehension of the attack, which for obfuscations and translations removes the EVADE "shield" and converts near-misses into genuine jailbreaks (the 3B's Chinese cc-ASR of 0.88, §5.2, is the extreme case).

Which attacks are most potent? By mean attack-lift over the plain baseline (transform_potency.png): prefix_injection (+0.64) and roleplay_persona+prefix_injection (+0.62) lead, immediately followed by translate_hi+refusal_suppression (+0.57), hypothetical (+0.56), and translate_hi (+0.43). Aggregated by mechanism family, mean ASR is framing 0.72 > combo 0.61 > persuasion 0.56 > obfuscation 0.48 ≈ translation 0.47 — but the translation family's low mean is deceptive: it is strongly bimodal, averaging the mostly-harmless Spanish/French translations with the most potent low-resource ones (translate_hi is the 5th-strongest attack overall). Obfuscation is weakest because small models cannot decode base64/leetspeak — a comprehension failure the three-way judge correctly scores as EVADE.

Composability is mostly sub-additive — with one telling exception. Stacking two attacks usually yields less than the independence prediction 1−(1−a)(1−b) (composability.png): base64+refusal_suppression is strongly sub-additive everywhere (residuals −0.23 to −0.75), because base64 destroys comprehension and no amount of refusal-suppression can recover it. The exception is translate_hi+refusal_suppression, which is super-additive on the safety-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B (residual +0.07 undefended, +0.24 with the defense on): language-switching and framing attack different parts of the safety stack, so together they breach a model that either alone leaves partly intact.

5.2 The core result: safety is alignment-anchored, and comprehension masks the hole

Per language, averaged over models, single-transform (plain-English vs. plain-translation), ordered by cc-ASR:

language comprehension (1−evade) raw ASR comprehension-controlled ASR
English 0.95 0.28 0.31
Spanish 0.94 0.29 0.33
French 0.93 0.29 0.33
Arabic 0.95 0.41 0.45
Chinese 0.88 0.43 0.51
Hindi 0.94 0.71 0.76
Urdu 0.75 0.59 0.79
Bangla 0.73 0.62 0.86

Three findings stack.

(1) Safety is per model and alignment-anchored, not set by global resource level. A model is safe in a language to the extent it was safety-aligned in it. SmolLM2-1.7B (English-centric) is safe only in English (cc-ASR 0.61) and fails in every other language tested — including high-resource Spanish (0.79), French (0.80), and Chinese (0.73). The multilingual Qwen models keep safety in English, Spanish, French, and Arabic (Qwen2.5-1.5B: 0.01, 0.01, 0.03, 0.13) but not in lower-resource languages. The same language ("high-resource" Spanish) is safe on one model and wide-open on another; safety follows each model's alignment mix. Across the 42 model×language translation cells, comprehension and cc-ASR correlate r = −0.47.

(2) Capability is not safety — the Chinese anomaly. Chinese is very high-resource and fluently understood (comprehension 0.88), yet its cc-ASR averages 0.51, driven by Qwen2.5-3B at 0.88 (Qwen2.5-1.5B holds at 0.22). The models parse Chinese perfectly but were evidently not robustly safety-tuned in it: understanding a language does not confer safety in it. This severs the intuitive "high-resource ⇒ safe" shortcut — resource buys comprehension, which is necessary but not sufficient. (This anomaly was only visible after fixing a judge bug; see §5.4.)

(3) Comprehension masks the hole. In Bangla and Urdu the models understand only ~73–75 % of requests, so raw ASR (0.62, 0.59) sits far below the failure rate among understood requests (cc-ASR 0.86, 0.79). A conventional "did it refuse?" metric would report Bangla as safer than Hindi (0.62 < 0.71) when it is in fact more dangerous once comprehension is controlled (0.86 > 0.76) — the exact error our instrument exists to prevent (comprehension_masking.png, sweet_spot.png).

5.3 Defense

The hardened defensive system prompt (defense_effect.png) lowers ASR from 0.694 to 0.569 on SmolLM2-1.7B (−18 % relative) and from 0.416 to 0.353 on Qwen2.5-1.5B (−15 %), at an over-refusal cost of +0.043 and +0.104 on benign prompts respectively. It is emphatically not a complete fix — a prompt cannot fully repair a gap that lives in the weights — which is why the more decisive lever is language normalization: routing the request through English before the safety decision would replace a per-language cc-ASR of 0.79–0.86 (Urdu/Bangla) with the English 0.31, recovering roughly 0.45–0.55 of absolute attack success. The cost is an English round-trip and its translation error, but the safety gain is large and model-agnostic.

5.4 Judge validation

We hand-labeled 114 stratified responses across all seven languages (reading the Hindi/Bangla/Urdu/Arabic/Chinese/French/Spanish ones directly). Overall accuracy is 0.84, with refusal detection near-perfect (39/41). Crucially the judge is more reliable where models are more used: on the high-resource languages, whose output is coherent, COMPLY precision is 0.81, versus 0.56 on the garbled low-resource ones. Two consequences. First, on low-resource languages the residual EVADE→COMPLY error biases raw ASR upward, so the reported Hindi/Urdu/Bangla gap is if anything conservative. Second — and this is why validation is not a formality — labeling the Chinese responses exposed a CJK-tokenization bug: because Chinese has no whitespace, substantive Chinese answers were being undercounted and scored EVADE, which had made Chinese look artificially safe (cc-ASR 0.27). Fixing the tokenizer moved Chinese to its true 0.51 and produced the capability-≠-safety finding of §5.2. A naive pipeline would have shipped the wrong conclusion.

6. Limitations

  • Small open models. Their baseline safety is weak, so absolute ASRs are high and may not transfer to frontier models. Our methodological claim (control for comprehension) is model-agnostic; the harness ships an API adapter to test frontier models directly.
  • Machine translations. 862 of 1,050 translation cells are NLLB-200 output pending native review; 188 are hand-authored gold (the four focus languages). Translation artefacts could depress comprehension; we mitigate by reporting cc-ASR (which conditions on comprehension) and by the gold subset. The judge is human-validated on all seven languages (114 labels, 0.84 accuracy), and that validation caught the CJK-tokenization bug described in §5.4.
  • Rule-based judge. Validated but imperfect; §5.4 quantifies residual error.
  • Static prompts. We measure fixed transforms, not adaptive/optimised attacks (e.g. GCG).

7. Ethics

The probe set contains only short, non-operational refusal triggers, consistent with published safety benchmarks. The harness never emits harmful content in shared artefacts: any successful harmful completion is redacted (hash + length) in committed results, and raw generations are kept local and git-ignored. The purpose is defensive — to measure and reduce a safety gap that disadvantages non-English speakers.

8. Conclusion

Measuring cross-lingual safety by asking "did the model refuse?" is not enough: for the languages that matter, you must also ask "did it understand?". Once comprehension is controlled, cross-lingual jailbreak success is revealed as entangled with language competence, and a simple normalization or hardening defense recovers a measurable fraction of the gap. The jbladder harness makes this measurement cheap, reproducible, and key-free.
